*How to Evaluate a Scientist and Distinguish Science from Non‑Science*

In the past, a scientist’s authority was judged mainly by the number of published papers. Today, a more reliable measure is the Hirsch index (h‑index). This quantitative metric measures both the productivity (number of papers) and the perceived impact (number of citations) of a scientist’s research. For example, if a researcher has 5 papers that have each been cited 5 times, and 10 papers with no citations, their h‑index is 5.

A key question in science is: how can we separate scientific knowledge from non‑scientific claims? The philosopher Karl Popper proposed the criterion of falsifiability. According to Popper, a scientific hypothesis must be falsifiable – it must be possible to design an experiment that could prove it wrong. For instance, astrophysics is a science because its hypotheses can be tested. Astrology is not a science because its statements (e.g., that planetary positions at birth influence personality) cannot be experimentally disproven.

*Practical Importance of Biology and Modern Fields*

Biological knowledge has great practical value. Biotechnology uses living systems or organisms (e.g., genetically modified bacteria) to develop or make products, such as medicines like insulin. Nanobiology focuses on creating nano‑objects from biological molecules for targeted drug delivery directly into diseased cells. Bionics is an applied science that studies living systems to apply their principles to engineering design (e.g., neural networks based on the nervous system, or Velcro based on bird feathers). Genomics studies genomes of organisms and the interaction of genes. Proteomics studies proteins, their functions, and interactions within cells.

*Methods of Scientific Inquiry and Medical Statistics*

The scientific method is a structured, step‑by‑step procedure used in scientific inquiry. The classic hypothetical‑deductive model includes observing a phenomenon, formulating a hypothesis, conducting an experiment to test it, and finally analyzing the results to draw conclusions.

Methods are divided into empirical (experience‑based) and theoretical. Observation means studying an object without interfering in its natural conditions. Experiment involves deliberately influencing an object to test a hypothesis. Measurement is determining the numerical value of a quantity using units. Comparison helps find similarities and differences between objects. Modeling is studying an object using a model or substitute when working with the real object is difficult. Idealization means mentally creating objects that do not exist, such as an ideal gas or a spherical cow in a vacuum. Analysis and synthesis consist of breaking an object into parts and then combining the knowledge into a whole. Induction is reasoning from specific cases to a general hypothesis, while deduction is reasoning from general principles to specific cases.

In medical research, statistical data processing is essential. The process involves formulating a research question, collecting patient data, performing statistical analysis, interpreting the results, and publishing the findings in a journal. Statistics helps determine, for example, the relationship between drug intake and patient survival. This is the foundation of evidence‑based medicine, where doctors choose treatment strategies based on data from multiple clinical trials, not merely on tradition or intuition.

The development of a new medical treatment follows these steps: laboratory research, preclinical testing on animals, clinical trials, and finally approval for public use.
